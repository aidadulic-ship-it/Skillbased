# 02 · Organizational Transformation

Editorial draft — source verification pending

**Learning outcome** — Design a pilot operating model and respond to evidence as conditions change.

**Skill** — Organizational Transformation. Assign decision rights and adapt a change pilot using evidence.

**Scope boundary** — Authority to change employment terms.

**Practice evidence** — Operating model and review memo.

## Lessons

### Structural, process and behavioral change

Map changes to decision rights, daily workflows and habits separately. An operating model is workable when the three reinforce each other during ordinary work.

**Example** — NOVA gives maintenance leads scheduling authority, adds a shadowing slot and trains supervisors to give observable feedback.

**Limitation** — A workflow change without time to practice can reinforce old habits.

**Knowledge check** — Which layer does granting scheduling authority change?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

Structure and decision rights.

</details>

### Operating model

Assign a decision owner, contributors, escalation path and review cadence for each recurring process. Specify who can change a skill definition and who can act on evidence.

**Example** — An HR editor maintains definitions while site leads approve operational use in their site.

**Limitation** — A single central owner may become a bottleneck if local exceptions accumulate.

**Knowledge check** — Who resolves a disagreement over pilot continuation?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

The named decision owner, with people holding safety or legal authority involved where relevant.

</details>

### Resistance as information

Treat objections as hypotheses about workload, fairness, authority or value. Ask for observations and adjust the pilot if the mechanism is credible.

**Example** — Operators report that recording evidence happens off shift. NOVA measures the burden and schedules paid documentation time.

**Limitation** — Some objections reveal a genuine conflict of incentives with no easy resolution.

**Knowledge check** — How can you test a workload objection?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

Observe the task, measure its time and check who must absorb it during peak demand.

</details>

### Readiness

Assess whether sponsors can allocate capacity, supervisors can coach and participants can correct records. Agree on prerequisites before expanding participation.

**Example** — One site starts after mentor coverage is confirmed while the other prepares training materials.

**Limitation** — Readiness varies across work units and can change after launch.

**Knowledge check** — What is a simple readiness gate?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

Scheduled mentor coverage and a tested path to correct inaccurate records.

</details>

### Six-month pilot roadmap

Sequence diagnosis, preparation, a bounded trial and review. At each gate, compare outcome data with guardrails and choose continue, redesign or stop.

**Example** — NOVA begins with two weeks of baseline observation, then adjusts scheduling after the first monthly review.

**Limitation** — Seasonality or equipment upgrades can make before-and-after comparisons misleading.

**Knowledge check** — What would justify redesign instead of expansion?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

A promising outcome with weak evidence or a correctable breach of a predefined guardrail.

</details>

## Challenges

### Map stakeholder resistance

NOVA Manufacturing is fictional. It has 180 staff at two sites and is considering a six-month skills pilot during modernization. HR and manager capacity are limited.

Supervisors report workload issues while employees say the purpose of evidence collection is unclear.

**Work product** — A stakeholder map with concerns, evidence requests and responses.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Ask what each person would need to see to support the change.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Review criteria · Rubric 1.0</summary>

- **Decision quality** — Treat objections as testable information. Reconsider your approach. Ask what each person would need to see to support the change.
- **Evidence quality** — Use scenario evidence and flag important missing information. Identify a specific observation and explain what remains uncertain.
- **Tradeoffs and next step** — Name a tradeoff, an owner and a review condition. State who acts, what might go wrong and when the decision should be revisited.

</details>

### Assign decision rights

NOVA Manufacturing is fictional. It has 180 staff at two sites and is considering a six-month skills pilot during modernization. HR and manager capacity are limited.

Two NOVA sites disagree over who may redefine troubleshooting evidence.

**Work product** — A decision-rights table with a named escalation path.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Distinguish proposing, approving and using a definition.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Review criteria · Rubric 1.0</summary>

- **Decision quality** — Assign one accountable approver and preserve version history. Reconsider your approach. Distinguish proposing, approving and using a definition.

</details>

### Review a failing pilot

NOVA Manufacturing is fictional. It has 180 staff at two sites and is considering a six-month skills pilot during modernization. HR and manager capacity are limited.

The pilot improved observed diagnosis time, but overtime exceeds its guardrail.

**Work product** — A continue, redesign or stop memo with criteria and an owner.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Consider whether a change in shift scheduling can restore the guardrail.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Review criteria · Rubric 1.0</summary>

- **Decision quality** — Review the guardrail and decide explicitly. Reconsider your approach. Consider whether a change in shift scheduling can restore the guardrail.
- **Evidence quality** — Use scenario evidence and flag important missing information. Identify a specific observation and explain what remains uncertain.
- **Tradeoffs and next step** — Name a tradeoff, an owner and a review condition. State who acts, what might go wrong and when the decision should be revisited.

</details>
