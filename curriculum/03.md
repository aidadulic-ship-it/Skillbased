# 03 · Skills Taxonomy Design

Editorial draft — source verification pending

**Learning outcome** — Build a small vocabulary for one decision and maintain its history.

**Skill** — Skills Taxonomy Design. Define and maintain a controlled skills vocabulary.

**Scope boundary** — Comprehensive occupational standards.

**Practice evidence** — Versioned vocabulary.

## Lessons

### Taxonomy and ontology

A taxonomy groups concepts. An ontology adds explicit relationships and constraints such as prerequisite or applies-to. Choose the simplest structure that supports a real query.

**Example** — NOVA groups diagnostic skills by task family and adds prerequisites only for supervised training assignments.

**Limitation** — More relationships increase maintenance work and can misrepresent loose associations.

**Knowledge check** — When is a hierarchy enough?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

When users primarily need consistent grouping and browsing.

</details>

### Granularity

Split a skill if separate evidence changes a practical decision. Merge labels if they share the same evidence, context and intended use.

**Example** — Separate electrical isolation from interpreting alarms because authorization and evidence requirements differ.

**Limitation** — A split can become too narrow to use across different equipment.

**Knowledge check** — What justifies a different skill rather than a synonym?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

A meaningful difference in the evidence or decision the labels support.

</details>

### Skill statements

Use an observable verb, object and relevant conditions. Write a boundary describing closely related activity that the statement does not cover.

**Example** — Interpret alarm histories for a named equipment family, excluding authority to restart machinery.

**Limitation** — Excessive detail can confuse a task checklist with a transferable skill.

**Knowledge check** — Improve the label maintenance expertise.

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

Interpret alarm histories on the relevant equipment and identify a safe escalation point.

</details>

### References and identifiers

Assign stable identifiers and record aliases, language and mappings as metadata. Preserve the local definition when an external term is only a partial match.

**Example** — ATLAS maps two site labels to one identifier only after comparing the actual evidence required.

**Limitation** — Names that translate similarly may encode different scope across regions.

**Knowledge check** — What should remain stable after a label change?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

The identifier, provided the underlying concept remains the same.

</details>

### Versioning and governance

Specify who approves changes, how versions are published and how evidence references are retained. Record replacement links for retired concepts.

**Example** — A revised diagnostic definition affects new assessments while earlier evidence still refers to version one.

**Limitation** — A crosswalk may be incomplete when a broad concept splits into several narrower ones.

**Knowledge check** — Why keep a prior definition?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

It explains the criteria under which historical evidence was collected.

</details>

## Challenges

### Classify maintenance terms

NOVA Manufacturing is fictional. It has 180 staff at two sites and is considering a six-month skills pilot during modernization. HR and manager capacity are limited.

An inventory mixes job names, shift duties, tasks and generic capabilities.

**Work product** — A classified list with ambiguous terms flagged.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Classify by contribution to work and evidence.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Review criteria · Rubric 1.0</summary>

- **Decision quality** — Flag terms with multiple meanings and clarify context. Reconsider your approach. Classify by contribution to work and evidence.
- **Evidence quality** — Use scenario evidence and flag important missing information. Identify a specific observation and explain what remains uncertain.
- **Tradeoffs and next step** — Name a tradeoff, an owner and a review condition. State who acts, what might go wrong and when the decision should be revisited.

</details>

### Draft a maintenance taxonomy

NOVA Manufacturing is fictional. It has 180 staff at two sites and is considering a six-month skills pilot during modernization. HR and manager capacity are limited.

NOVA needs a way to select supervised diagnostic practice.

**Work product** — Five skill records with IDs, boundaries and evidence types.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Limit scope to the practice decisions the site makes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Review criteria · Rubric 1.0</summary>

- **Decision quality** — Use observable statements and stable IDs. Reconsider your approach. Limit scope to the practice decisions the site makes.
- **Evidence quality** — Use scenario evidence and flag important missing information. Identify a specific observation and explain what remains uncertain.
- **Tradeoffs and next step** — Name a tradeoff, an owner and a review condition. State who acts, what might go wrong and when the decision should be revisited.

</details>

### Simplify a complex taxonomy

ATLAS Global is fictional. It has 30,000 staff, regional operating differences and shared taxonomy governance challenges.

ATLAS has overlapping terms that return inconsistent search results.

**Work product** — A merge, split and retire proposal with a historical mapping.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Protect evidence attached to deprecated labels.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Review criteria · Rubric 1.0</summary>

- **Decision quality** — Preserve histories and test mappings. Reconsider your approach. Protect evidence attached to deprecated labels.
- **Evidence quality** — Use scenario evidence and flag important missing information. Identify a specific observation and explain what remains uncertain.
- **Tradeoffs and next step** — Name a tradeoff, an owner and a review condition. State who acts, what might go wrong and when the decision should be revisited.

</details>
